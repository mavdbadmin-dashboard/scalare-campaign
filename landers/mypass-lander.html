Shell Mandated It. Chevron Adopted It. Retail Investors Have One Way In.

MyPass Global has become essential infrastructure for some of the world's largest energy operations. The company is not publicly listed. Scalare Partners is.

By James Alderton-Hale  |  Special to Pacific Capital Report
September 16, 2026  ·  4 min read

Workforce compliance in heavy industry has become a critical risk management function — and a growing software market. | Photo: Unsplash

Before a contractor can step onto an offshore platform operated by one of the world's major energy companies, a significant amount of paperwork must be verified. Safety certifications, equipment licences, industry-specific training records, medical clearances, and site induction documentation — a portfolio of credentials that varies by employer, by jurisdiction, and by worksite. In practice, verifying all of it is done manually, slowly, and inconsistently. The consequences of getting it wrong can be catastrophic.

In sectors where a single safety failure can result in loss of life, shutdown of a billion-dollar asset, or nine-figure regulatory liability, the appetite for better systems is not a technology trend — it is a business imperative. The global workforce compliance software market is estimated at $60 billion and growing, driven by tightening regulatory frameworks in energy, mining, construction, and logistics. The scramble to automate what humans are doing badly with spreadsheets and filing cabinets is accelerating.

MyPass Global, founded in Brisbane and now operating across Australia, the United Kingdom, and North America, has built the centralised credentialing and workforce compliance platform that some of the world's most rigorous industrial operators have chosen to trust. The company's platform digitises the entire lifecycle of workforce compliance — credential issuance, verification, expiry tracking, and site access management — and presents it through a unified interface accessible to employers, contractors, and workers simultaneously.

What distinguishes MyPass from the dozens of HR-technology vendors who pitch workforce management tools is the mechanism by which it grows. When Shell mandates a credentialing standard across its contractor network, every contractor working for Shell has to adopt the platform. When Chevron and Santos follow, the adoption is not voluntary — it is embedded in the conditions of engagement. This mandate-led growth model transforms sales into something more structural: compliance becomes a condition of employment, and MyPass becomes the infrastructure through which that compliance is administered.

"When the world's largest energy companies mandate your product, you don't need a sales team. You need a customer success team."
— Venture capital investor, energy technology sector

The software analogy closest to this dynamic may be Xero, the New Zealand-based accounting platform that grew not through aggressive enterprise sales but through accountant mandates. When a mid-sized accounting firm told its client base to migrate to Xero, hundreds of small businesses had to comply. The network effect was institutional, not viral. MyPass is running a structurally similar playbook in industrial compliance — and the results in retention and expansion revenue reflect it.

Investor confidence arrived formally in February 2025, when OneVentures — the Sydney-based deep technology fund with portfolio companies spanning medtech, artificial intelligence, and industrial software — led a Series A funding round in MyPass Global. OneVentures' investment thesis is grounded in backing companies with defensible technical differentiation in large, under-served markets. Its MyPass investment signals a view that workforce compliance in heavy industry is neither niche nor saturated.

The execution metrics reinforce that view. In a single month of product development last reported publicly, the company shipped 27 separate product releases — a velocity of iteration that reflects an engineering culture aligned with the urgency of its compliance-software category. In enterprise software, where customers make three-to-five-year technology decisions, the compounding advantage of a faster release cadence is difficult to overstate.

The market the company is addressing is not theoretical. The $60 billion workforce compliance software segment is driven by increasingly mandatory regulatory frameworks across every major jurisdiction in which MyPass operates. In Australia, Safe Work legislation is becoming more prescriptive. In the UK, post-Grenfell construction regulation has elevated credentialing requirements across all licensed contractors. In the US, OSHA enforcement is intensifying. These are tailwinds that no change of management or macroeconomic cycle is likely to reverse.

The industrial world is running on paper-based compliance that was designed for a different era. The companies that replace it — and become embedded in the operating contracts of the largest energy majors on earth — will not be easy to displace. MyPass, by virtue of its mandate relationship with Shell, Chevron, and Santos, has already crossed the threshold that most enterprise software companies spend years trying to reach.

The question is not whether the category grows. The question is who captures it.
