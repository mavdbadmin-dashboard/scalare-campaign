Wise Built a £10 Billion Business From This Exact Idea. An Australian Company Is Running the Same Play.

Master Remit is building the dominant cross-border payment platform for migrant communities in the Asia-Pacific. The parallels to early-stage Wise are striking.

By Priya Nandakumar  |  Special to Asia-Pacific Financial Observer
September 16, 2026  ·  4 min read

The global remittance market moves more than $200 trillion annually, yet most migrants still pay 5–8% in transfer fees. | Photo: Unsplash

Maria Santos has worked as a registered nurse in Sydney for six years. Every fortnight, within hours of her pay landing in her Commonwealth Bank account, she sends a portion of it home to her family in Cebu. For most of those six years, that transfer passed through a major bank, cost her somewhere between five and eight percent of the total, and arrived three to five business days later. She had no better option she trusted.

Maria's situation is not unusual. There are more than 1.2 million Filipino-born residents in Australia. Across the Asia-Pacific, tens of millions of migrant workers — nurses, engineers, construction workers, domestic staff — send money home through corridors that are antiquated, expensive, and largely unchanged since the mid-1990s. The global remittance market moves an estimated $208 trillion annually, and the digital segment alone is projected to reach $60 billion by 2030. The opportunity is large. The incumbents are slow. The window for a better-positioned challenger is open.

TransferWise — now rebranded as Wise — saw this window in 2011. Founded by two Estonian friends frustrated by the opaque fees of international bank transfers, the company built a peer-to-peer matching system that eliminated the need for correspondent banking rails and passed the savings on to users. By the time it listed on the London Stock Exchange in 2021, it was valued at £8.75 billion. Today, Wise processes billions in monthly volume and holds a dominant share of the UK-to-Europe corridor.

The question Wise answered for Europe and North America — who builds the trusted, low-cost remittance layer for the migrant economy? — remains substantially unanswered in the Asia-Pacific. That is the gap Master Remit has identified and is systematically filling.

"The loyalty of migrants who find a platform that finally treats them fairly is extraordinary. Churn is almost non-existent."
— Fintech investor, Asia-Pacific remittance sector

Master Remit, the Sydney-based cross-border payments company, has built a digital platform specifically designed for the migrant corridors that run between Australia, New Zealand, and the economies of Southeast Asia and the Pacific Islands. The service is faster, cheaper, and more transparent than traditional banking alternatives. More importantly, it is built by a team that understands, culturally and structurally, who its users are and what trust means to them in a financial context.

The comparable set is instructive. Wise was dismissed for years as a niche product before it became a category-defining company. Remitly, the Seattle-based digital remittance firm, reached a $2.4 billion valuation on Nasdaq by focusing on specific corridors with cultural precision. Afterpay, before it became a global brand, was a buy-now-pay-later service addressing a very specific friction point for a very specific demographic in Australia. The pattern is consistent: identify a financial pain point in an underserved community, build trust one corridor at a time, and expand as the network effects compound.

Master Remit's execution reflects this discipline. The company has processed more than 150,000 transactions, moving in excess of $50 million through its platform. It recently launched six new Asian remittance corridors, expanding its addressable market and deepening its routing infrastructure across the region's highest-volume migrant flows. Each new corridor adds both revenue and data — which improves fraud detection, pricing optimisation, and the user experience for the next corridor launched.

The retention numbers tell the deeper story. Migrant communities, once they find a payments platform that is honest about fees and reliable about delivery, tend not to leave. Transfer timing is a social commitment — a daughter's school fees depend on it, a family's rent may depend on it. The emotional stakes of a failed or delayed transfer are high. The trust earned by a platform that delivers consistently is correspondingly durable. Customer acquisition in remittances is expensive; customer retention, once achieved, is remarkably low-cost.

The competitive moat is also structural. Building compliant cross-border payment infrastructure requires regulatory licensing across multiple jurisdictions, banking partnerships on both ends of each corridor, and anti-money-laundering systems capable of satisfying the requirements of AUSTRAC in Australia, central bank regulators in the Philippines, Indonesia, Fiji, and increasingly more countries in the region. These are not obstacles that a new entrant can clear quickly. Master Remit has already cleared them.

For retail investors, the challenge is access. Master Remit is not separately listed on any exchange. The company's fundraising has been conducted through private channels, accessible to a small number of institutional and high-net-worth investors. The retail participation gap — between knowing a company exists and being able to invest in it at a meaningful stage — is the structural problem that listed venture vehicles are designed to address.

That is the customer. That is the market. That is the play.
